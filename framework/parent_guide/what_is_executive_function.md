# What Executive Function Is

## The everyday version

Executive function is the set of brain skills a person uses to run themselves. Five of them cover most of what you notice at home:

- **Getting started** on something that is not urgent and not fun.
- **Sustaining effort** past the point where it stops being interesting.
- **Knowing when to stop**, which includes stopping a task that is finished and stopping one that is going nowhere.
- **Staying organized**, so the thing you need is where you can find it.
- **Being flexible**, so a plan that stops working gets changed instead of abandoned.

These skills develop on their own schedule, and that schedule is not the same as a child's reading age or their intelligence. A child who reads two grades ahead can still need a nudge to start every single time.

## Why it matters past this trip

Almost every hard thing a school-aged child is asked to do runs on these five. A homework assignment with a two-week deadline is a getting-started problem. A chore nobody enjoys is a sustaining-effort problem. A science project is all five at once.

That is why the trip is the hook and not the subject.

## Why this project is a promising way to build them

Three things make this a better practice ground than a worksheet.

It is a real task. The trip actually happens, the child's recommendation actually feeds a family decision, and an adult actually books something at the end of it. Effort on something that matters is easier to sustain.

It is long. The project runs for months, in short sessions, which is the shape these skills are built in. A single afternoon of planning trains nothing.

It is bridged. Every session names the planning move out loud and asks where else the child could use it. That naming is deliberate, and the next section is about why.

## The honest part

Transfer is not guaranteed. A child who gets good at planning a trip does not automatically get good at planning a school project, and the research on that is mixed.

What raises the odds is explicit bridging: saying the name of the move at the moment the child uses it. "You just broke a big thing into one small first step. That is the same move your book report needs." Said often enough, by you, in ordinary language.

So do that part, and do not expect a generalized payoff. This project is worth doing for the trip and the practice. Anything that carries over is earned by the naming rather than promised by the design.
