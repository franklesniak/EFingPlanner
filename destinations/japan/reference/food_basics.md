# Food Basics (Japan)

**Last reviewed:** September 2026

This page is for your food research. It sorts the places people eat by kind, explains how ordering works, and names what a family plans ahead. Picking the actual places is your research, and grown-ups check prices and book.

## Kinds of places to eat

- **Conveyor-belt sushi (kaitenzushi)** -- plates of sushi ride past on a moving belt, and you take the ones you want. Many places also take orders on a screen at your seat.
- **Noodle shops** -- ramen, udon and soba, usually quick and casual. Many have a counter to sit at.
- **Department-store food halls (depachika)** -- a whole floor of food counters, often in the basement. They sell lunch boxes, sweets, fruit and snacks to take away.
- **Convenience stores (konbini)** -- rice balls, sandwiches, hot snacks and drinks, open long hours. Plenty of people in Japan eat from them every day, and they're a good option for a family.
- **Set-meal restaurants (teishoku)** -- one tray with a main dish, rice, soup and small sides.
- **Izakaya** -- a casual pub-restaurant with small plates to share. It's mostly an evening place built around drinks, so grown-ups decide whether one fits your family.

## How ordering works

- **Ticket machines** -- at many noodle and set-meal places, you buy a ticket from a machine by the door. Then you hand it to the staff. The buttons often have pictures.
- **Food models and photo menus** -- many places show their dishes as plastic models in the window, or as pictures on the menu. You can point to what you want.
- **Screens at the table** -- some places take orders on a tablet, often with an English setting.
- **Paying** -- at many places, you pay at the counter by the door as you leave. How people pay, with cash or a card, is on the [money basics page](money_basics.md).

The words for ordering and asking for the bill are on the [language basics page](language_basics.md).

## Planning ahead

Two things take planning when your group is mixed:

- **Food needs.** Allergies, vegetarian meals and other food needs are worth checking before the trip. Many dishes use *dashi*, a soup stock usually made with fish, even when they look meat-free. A grown-up checks ahead for anything that matters. A card that explains the need in Japanese can help.
- **Seats for a group.** Many small restaurants seat only a few people. A bigger group may need to book ahead, look for a larger place, or split into two tables.

## Everyday food is part of the trip

Some of the best food moments cost little. A rice ball from a convenience store. A drink from a vending machine. A station lunch box (*ekiben*) eaten on the train. A slow walk through a food hall. These are good food experiences in their own right, and they're part of what eating in Japan is like. Famous restaurants are one kind of good food among many.
