# Etiquette Basics (Japan)

**Last reviewed:** September 2026

These are the everyday ways things are done in Japan. They're how the place works, the same way leaving a tip at a restaurant is how things work in the United States. People are kind to visitors. If you get one wrong, someone will usually show you kindly, and everyone moves on.

## Shoes

In homes, people take their shoes off at the door. Traditional inns, some restaurants and some temple halls ask for it too. Look for a step up at the entrance, or a row of shoes or slippers.

Slippers are often set out for you. Some places keep a separate pair just for the bathroom, and you swap back when you come out. On tatami mats, people walk in socks or bare feet.

## Saying hello

A small nod or bow is an everyday way to say hello, and to say thank you. From a visitor, a small nod and a smile are plenty.

## On trains and buses

Trains and buses are quiet. People talk softly, and phones are set to silent. Phone calls wait until you're off the train. On long-distance trains like the Shinkansen, eating a packed lunch at your seat is normal.

## Lines

People line up for trains, buses, shops and popular places to eat. On train platforms, marks on the floor show where to wait. When the train comes, let people get off first, then get on.

## Trash

Public trash cans can be hard to find. Many people carry a small bag and take their trash back to where they're staying. Where there are trash cans, they're split by type, such as burnable trash, plastic bottles and cans. Convenience stores often have trash cans for things bought there.

## Handing over money

Many shop counters have a small tray by the register. Put your money in the tray, and the cashier takes it from there. Your change may come back in the tray, or handed to you.

Tipping is uncommon in Japan, at restaurants, in taxis and at hotels. So pay just the amount on the bill. How people pay, with cash or a card, is on the [money basics page](money_basics.md).

## Shrines and temples

Shrines are places of worship in the Shinto religion. Temples are Buddhist. Visitors are welcome at most of them.

- At a shrine gate, called a *torii*, many people give a small bow. They walk a little to one side of the middle of the path.
- A water basin near the entrance is for rinsing your hands before you pray. Signs often show the steps.
- Shrines and temples each have their own way to pray. At a shrine, people often bow twice, clap twice, and bow once more. At a temple, people press their hands together and pray quietly.
- Speak softly. Some halls ask you to take off your shoes, or to put your camera away. Follow the signs.

## Photos

You can take photos in most places. Some places limit them: some temple halls, museums, shops and private homes, and some neighborhoods. In parts of Kyoto's Gion district, for example, posted signs ask visitors to stay off private lanes and to put cameras away there.

Watch for posted signs, and do what they say. Ask before you take a photo of a person.

## Bathing

Public baths and hot-spring baths are part of everyday life in Japan. You'll hear about two kinds:

- **Onsen** -- a bath filled with natural hot-spring water. Many are in hot-spring towns or at traditional inns.
- **Sento** -- a neighborhood public bathhouse, with heated tap water. Locals use them the way you might use a town pool.

Shared baths have their own rules:

- People bathe nude, so swimsuits stay packed.
- Baths are usually split into a side for men and a side for women.
- Everyone washes and rinses off at a shower seat before getting into the shared water.
- While they soak, people keep the small towel on the edge of the bath, or folded on their head.
- Many baths limit visible tattoos. The usual ways around that are a cover-up sticker for a small tattoo, a private bath, or a bath that welcomes tattoos.

**Age rules differ from bath to bath.** Children past a certain age use the side for their own gender. The age is set locally, area by area, and each bath posts its own rule. It's often somewhere in the early grade-school years, and a bath can change it, so read the sign at the bath you visit. Whether and how a child your age takes part is for the grown-ups to decide.

A family that wants to bathe together can look for a private bath, called a *kashikiri* bath. It's booked for a set time, just for your group. Many inns and some hot-spring towns have them.
